Enterprise AI infrastructure · 26 September 2026

You found the GPUs.
Now comes the hard part.

The deal looks compelling. The hardware is available. Your researchers are ready. But who will make this new cloud work inside the business you already run?

You've found the capacity your AI team has been asking for. Perhaps CoreWeave has the right cluster. Perhaps Nebius offers the region or economics you need. Perhaps Crusoe has hardware your existing provider cannot supply when you need it.

For the first time in months, the compute problem looks solvable.

Then your platform engineering lead asks: “How does this work with everything we already have?”

Your data is in AWS, Azure or Google Cloud. Your developers use established tools. Your security team has spent years getting identity, permissions and audit under control. Your platform engineers already support more systems than they have time for.

Now the business wants another cloud.

The provider can deliver the GPUs. It cannot automatically make them part of your organisation.

This is the risk hidden inside an otherwise sound infrastructure decision. It is also the opportunity: get the integration right, and the next compute decision becomes much easier.

The savings are real. So is the cost of reaching them.

Specialist GPU providers can offer better pricing, more capacity, newer hardware and geographic choice. For the right workload, the economics are compelling. But the number on the rate card is not the number your business experiences.

Imagine committing to a cluster because it promises substantial savings. The contract starts. The GPUs are ready. Then the work begins.

Security needs to approve the network. Engineers must connect your identity provider. Training data needs a secure path into the environment. Monitoring is incomplete. Nobody has agreed who takes the call when a job fails at 2am.

Meanwhile, you're paying for capacity your researchers cannot fully use.

The provider has delivered what it sold. Your business hasn't yet realised what it bought.

The right calculation isn't simply cost per GPU-hour. It's cost per useful, completed workload, including data movement, utilisation, engineering effort and ongoing operations. Make that calculation before an attractive infrastructure quote becomes a disappointing business case.

Your platform team inherits the problem.

The request sounds small: establish connectivity, configure access and get a training job running.

But a researcher needs to sign in through your existing identity system. The workload needs permission to read your data without embedding permanent credentials. Security needs to see who accessed what. Finance needs to attribute spend. Operations needs to distinguish an application failure from a network or provider incident.

For a regulated business, add data location, administrative access, audit retention and recovery.

These are familiar engineering problems. The difficult part is making them work together across infrastructure you don't fully control, without creating a second operating model your team must maintain forever.

The boundary that matters: Your provider supplies its GPUs, networking, storage, APIs and contracted managed services. Your organisation must still connect those services to its access model, approved data flows, workload delivery, cost ownership and operations. The exact division varies by provider and contract. Establish it before the first incident, not during it.

The first security review is where the demo meets the enterprise.

A researcher can train a model on a new cluster with a small dataset and temporary credentials. That proves technical feasibility. It doesn't establish readiness for sensitive workloads.

Imagine a financial firm evaluating EU-hosted compute. The hardware fits. The research team is happy. Then security asks where the checkpoints go. Legal asks whether support staff outside the region can access the environment. Platform engineering asks how workloads authenticate to existing data. Operations asks where the audit logs will live.

“The GPUs are in the EU” doesn't answer those questions. Nor does a provider's compliance certificate replace your own architecture review.

Providers are improving. CoreWeave has published enterprise identity and key-control capabilities. But those features still need to be integrated with your groups, workload permissions, network policies and audit requirements.

Don't create a permanent security exception for AI. Bring new compute under the controls your business already trusts.

Imagine your researchers barely notice the new cloud.

They request the compute they need and submit jobs through a familiar workflow. Identity, approved data access, quotas and cost ownership are handled by the platform. They can still use provider-specific features when their work requires them, but they don't have to become experts in every cloud's authentication and networking just to run an experiment.

Shopify has published how it extended SkyPilot to support GPU workloads across Google Cloud and Nebius. The important work wasn't just sending jobs to different clusters. Shopify expressed its own scheduling, configuration and cost-ownership requirements as reusable platform capabilities.

A scheduler can send a job to another cloud. Your platform must make that job fit your organisation.

The tool might be SkyPilot, your existing job templates or another orchestration approach. The outcome matters more: researchers get useful compute without creating a new integration project every time.

The second provider reveals what you really built.

Suppose your first integration succeeds. Six months later, another team wants different GPUs. A new provider offers better capacity, a more suitable region or a price that changes the economics.

Do you repeat the entire exercise? Another identity configuration. Another deployment process. Another monitoring system. Another cost report. Another operational burden.

Or can you extend what you've already built?

The advantage isn't access to five GPU clouds. It's being able to introduce the next one without rebuilding your engineering organisation around it.

This doesn't mean every workload should be portable. Large training jobs depend on particular hardware, interconnects, storage and data locality. Moving them indiscriminately can destroy the savings you hoped to achieve.

What should be reusable is your organisation's ability to evaluate, approve, deploy, observe and operate suitable new infrastructure.

Get one real workload into production. Then make the next one easier.

The opposite mistake is to recognise this challenge and design an elaborate multi-cloud platform before anyone has run a useful workload. Start smaller, with an outcome the business can recognise.

  1. Choose the workload that justifies the move. Is the goal lower completed-job cost, faster experiments, available capacity, new hardware or an approved processing location? Establish a baseline.
  2. Resolve what could stop it. Identify identity, security, networking, data and residency requirements while they can still influence provider selection and contract terms.
  3. Connect it to your existing operating model. Reuse established developer tools, access controls, monitoring and incident response. Build the missing integration, not another organisation.
  4. Prove the result under real conditions. Run the actual workload. Measure useful throughput, total cost, reliability and the effort required to support it.
  5. Keep what makes the next move easier. Turn proven identity patterns, job interfaces, cost attribution and operational practices into reusable capabilities. Add wider orchestration only when demand warrants it.

The first milestone isn't “we have a multi-cloud platform.” It's “our researchers can run this workload securely, economically and reliably, and our existing team can support it.”

The next infrastructure advantage is the ability to absorb change.

New GPU hardware will arrive. Capacity and pricing will move. Providers will expand their regions and enterprise services. Those developments create opportunity only for organisations that can act on them.

If every new provider requires months of engineering, your choices are constrained by your integration backlog. If you have a proven way to introduce compute, you can choose based on the workload, the economics and the business requirement.

The GPUs are the purchase. The ability to put them to work inside your business is the asset.
