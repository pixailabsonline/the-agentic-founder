Enterprise AI infrastructure · 26 September 2026

You found the GPUs.
Now comes the hard part.

The capacity is available. The business case looks compelling. Your researchers are ready. The risk is discovering too late that your organisation cannot put the GPUs to work.

You've found the capacity your AI team has been asking for. Perhaps Nebius has the cluster, regional footprint or commercial terms your workload needs. Perhaps CoreWeave has the right capacity and managed services. Perhaps Crusoe offers hardware or deployment options your existing provider cannot supply when you need them. The choice depends on your workload, region, contract and existing estate, not a preferred vendor.

For the first time in months, the compute problem looks solvable.

Then your platform engineering lead asks: "How does this work with everything we already have?"

That question exposes the risk in the investment case. A good GPU deal can still disappoint if identity, data movement, security approval and operating responsibilities delay the first productive workload. The integration surface is larger than a provider's rate card can show, and its cost depends on your existing estate.

This is the buyer's journey from promising GPU capacity to a workload your organisation can securely run, measure and support. Along the way, it maps the engineering decisions that determine whether the original business case survives production. I draw on more than twenty years of software and infrastructure engineering in demanding environments, alongside documented provider capabilities and published implementations. The scenarios below are illustrative, not accounts of a particular client's deployment.

The provider's responsibilities vary by service and contract. Some offer substantial managed identity, networking, storage, monitoring and recovery capabilities; others leave more to the customer. Your task is to identify what your chosen provider supplies, what must connect to your existing estate and who owns each failure mode. The diagram is an integration checklist, not a claim that every provider stops at a Kubernetes API.

The GPU deal isn't the business outcome. A productive workload is.

Specialist GPU providers can offer better pricing, more capacity, newer hardware and geographic choice. For the right workload, the economics are compelling. But the number on the rate card is not the number your business experiences.

Imagine committing to a cluster because it promises substantial savings. The contract starts. The GPUs are ready. Then the work begins.

Security needs to approve the network path. Engineers must federate your identity provider. Training data needs a secure route into the environment. Monitoring is incomplete. Nobody has agreed who takes the call when a job fails at 2am.

Meanwhile, you're paying for capacity your researchers cannot fully use.

The provider has delivered what it sold. Your business hasn't yet realised what it bought.

The right calculation isn't cost per GPU-hour. It's cost per useful, completed workload — including data movement, integration engineering, utilisation losses and ongoing operations. Before committing, establish a baseline for the workload, the integration work required, a realistic time to production and the cost of capacity that may sit idle while your team gets there. A cheaper GPU is only a saving when the complete workload economics support it.

The first wall: your identity system stops at the new cloud.

Your researchers already sign in through Microsoft Entra, Okta or another established identity provider. Their groups determine what they can access. Security can disable accounts and review activity. A separate GPU-cloud account might get the first experiment running, but what happens when twenty researchers join, one leaves or auditors need six months of access history?

Start with your requirements, then test each provider against them. CoreWeave publishes enterprise identity and provisioning material. Nebius documents SAML-based SSO, service accounts and IAM. Crusoe publishes security and identity information for its enterprise offerings. Public documentation differs in depth; that is not evidence that one provider lacks an undocumented capability. Request a technical demonstration for your exact service, region and contract.

Ask each provider the same questions: Which SAML or OIDC flows are supported? How are groups provisioned and deprovisioned? What are the RBAC boundaries for Kubernetes or Slurm? Which conditional-access controls survive federation? How are administrative actions logged? Can your existing SIEM receive the relevant events?

Then test the identity that matters after the researcher signs in: the workload itself. A training job on Nebius, CoreWeave or Crusoe that reads an S3 bucket needs an approved way to authenticate. Long-lived cloud keys embedded in containers are an avoidable risk. Where the chosen service supports the required federation, use short-lived workload credentials; otherwise design a tightly controlled, rotated alternative.

Your secrets manager, audit-log correlation and break-glass process must work across the boundary too. Kubernetes External Secrets can help distribute secrets, but it does not make the trust decisions for you.

Acceptance test: onboard a researcher, run a workload under the correct permissions, revoke access, verify the logs and prove that no forgotten credentials retain access. Do this before treating identity integration as complete.

The second wall: the GPUs are ready, but your data is somewhere else.

Identity gets researchers through the door. Then the first real job needs its training data, perhaps terabytes sitting in your existing cloud. The network path, data placement and access pattern now determine whether your expensive GPUs spend their time computing or waiting.

There are three practical patterns. Replicate frequently used datasets near the compute for locality, accepting synchronisation, versioning and duplicate storage. Read remotely when data size and access patterns make that viable, measuring real throughput, latency, resilience and transfer costs. Stage data per workload for discrete experiments, with automated cleanup and a recorded dataset version.

Shopify's published use of SkyPilot with Google Cloud and Nebius is an instructive example of designing for data locality rather than assuming that every workload should read across clouds. It is an example of one company's workload and architecture, not a universal prescription.

Provider-specific connectivity and caching services may change the economics. CoreWeave documents cross-cloud connectivity and data-access products; Nebius and Crusoe have their own network, storage and connectivity options. Evaluate all three against the same data-flow diagram, measured workload throughput, geographic requirements and total transfer costs. Verify availability for the cloud pairing and region you actually need.

A VPN may get the first workload running. But can it sustain several teams moving large datasets? Do you need private connectivity? Can workloads resolve internal DNS? What happens when a transfer fails halfway through? Do the proposed network controls meet your security team's requirements?

For EU-sensitive workloads, an EU GPU region is only the beginning. Establish where datasets, checkpoints, model artefacts, logs and backups reside, which jurisdictions support staff can access them from and what the provider's subprocessor arrangements mean for your data. Residency is an end-to-end property of the workload, not a data-centre checkbox.

Acceptance test: run the actual data-loading pattern at expected scale, document the complete data flow and calculate the resulting cost per completed job before extrapolating from a benchmark.

If the GPUs are available but the data, identity or network is holding up your first workload, describe the integration blocker. Agentic Founder can scope the work required to reach production.

The first security review is where the demo meets the enterprise.

A researcher can train a model on a new cluster with a small dataset and temporary credentials. That proves technical feasibility. It doesn't establish readiness for sensitive workloads.

Imagine a financial firm evaluating EU-hosted compute. The hardware fits. The research team is happy. Then security asks where the checkpoints go. Legal asks whether support staff outside the region can access the environment. Platform engineering asks how workloads authenticate to existing data. Operations asks where the audit logs will live.

"The GPUs are in the EU" doesn't answer those questions. Nor does a provider's compliance certificate replace your own architecture review.

Providers are improving their enterprise capabilities. Nebius, CoreWeave and Crusoe each publish security, infrastructure and regional information, but their available features and contractual responsibilities differ by service. Compare the controls your workload requires, request current evidence and test the integration with your groups, workload permissions, network policies and audit requirements. A compliance certificate or an approved region is evidence for a particular scope, not blanket approval of your end-to-end architecture.

Don't create a permanent security exception for AI. Bring new compute under the controls your business already trusts.

Your researchers didn't ask for another cloud. They asked to get more work done.

Now the first job runs. Another twenty researchers want access. One team needs a different GPU type; another wants reserved capacity; a third wants to compare the same experiment across providers.

Giving everyone direct access shifts provider-specific authentication, storage, quotas and deployment work onto the people you were trying to accelerate.

Shopify's published implementation is instructive because it uses Nebius alongside Google Cloud. Its SkyPilot-based platform routes suitable GPU jobs to Nebius while incorporating Shopify's own scheduling, configuration and cost-ownership requirements. The lesson isn't that every enterprise needs Shopify's exact stack. It's that a general-purpose tool becomes a productive internal platform only after your organisation's policies are integrated.

These building blocks solve distinct problems:

  • SkyPilot: job submission and provider or cluster selection, where multi-environment routing is needed.
  • Kueue and MultiKueue: Kubernetes admission, quota management and supported multi-cluster dispatch.
  • Ray and KubeRay: distributed execution and management of Ray clusters on Kubernetes.
  • PyTorch Distributed and DeepSpeed: distributed training capabilities with their own execution and recovery trade-offs.

A stack might route a job to Nebius, CoreWeave or another suitable environment, admit it according to team quota and launch the required distributed runtime. But each layer must still respect enterprise identity, data locality and cost policy.

But don't introduce every abstraction on day one. A single provider and a small team may need only well-designed job templates. Quotas become important when teams compete for reserved GPUs. Cross-provider routing is useful when you have a real placement decision to automate.

Then finance asks a question your monitoring dashboard can't answer.

Researchers are running jobs. Utilisation is rising. Finance asks which team consumed the capacity and whether the new provider is delivering the expected savings.

The provider has billing data. Kubernetes shows resource usage. Your experiment tracker knows which jobs ran. Those systems may not agree on the cost of a completed experiment.

A job can occupy eight GPUs for ten hours while spending much of that time waiting on data. Reserved capacity may already be paid for. Restarts and failed experiments consume resources too. Across providers, billing, cluster telemetry and experiment records rarely line up without deliberate integration. Don't rely on a single GPU-utilisation percentage to establish the economics of a workload.

What you need to build:

  • A common labelling convention enforced at submission time, not after the fact.
  • Billing data aggregation from each provider into your cost management system.
  • Showback or chargeback that works across the combined estate.
  • Waste detection. Idle GPUs on a specialist cloud consume budget silently. Shopify's reaper approach — terminate jobs below a utilisation threshold — is a pattern worth adopting early.

Cost visibility should change behaviour. Researchers right-size jobs. Platform engineers identify idle capacity. Leadership compares completed-workload economics instead of competing rate cards.

The first serious failure reveals whether you have a platform.

A long training job fails overnight. Is it a GPU fault, an interrupted data path, exhausted storage or a provider incident? Can the job resume from a checkpoint? Is that checkpoint accessible in the recovery environment? Can the on-call engineer tell which organisation owns the next action?

Managed recovery and provider-side resilience features can reduce interruptions, but benchmarked goodput figures from different providers or workloads are not directly comparable. Ask Nebius, CoreWeave and Crusoe how the exact service handles node failures, checkpoint storage, capacity replacement and escalation. Test recovery with your own workload.

When your workload spans your estate and a provider's infrastructure, recovery requires explicitly agreed responsibilities:

  • Checkpointing. Your training code must checkpoint at intervals that balance recovery time against I/O overhead. The checkpoint must be stored somewhere accessible for recovery — ideally not only on the provider's local storage.
  • Automatic recovery. SkyPilot's managed spot jobs can recover preempted work on a different cluster. This requires idempotent jobs, accessible data and a configured recovery path.
  • Capacity fallback. If the specialist provider's capacity is exhausted, can the job move to your existing cloud? Only if the right GPU types are available, the data is accessible and the environment is configured. Most organisations can't do this without significant preparation.
  • Partial failure in distributed jobs. A multi-node training run where one node fails is different from a single-node preemption. PyTorch Distributed, DeepSpeed and Ray have different fault tolerance capabilities. Some can recover from partial failures; others require a full restart.

Key decision: What is your recovery time objective for interrupted GPU workloads? A 24-hour training run that restarts from scratch is a 24-hour loss. A job that resumes from a checkpoint loses minutes. The difference is engineering investment in checkpointing, storage and automated recovery — and that investment must happen before the first interruption.

Day-two operations extend well beyond failure recovery. GPU driver and CUDA version compatibility, certificate rotation, VPN tunnel maintenance, node pool management, provider API changes, abandoned storage cleanup, monitoring integration — each is a recurring maintenance task your team inherits. Provider dashboards are valuable, but your team needs a joined-up picture of application, scheduler, GPU, storage, network and identity across both environments.

The pattern we see repeatedly: teams that invest heavily in getting the first workload running, then discover they've built nothing reusable for the second. If you're approaching your first specialist GPU integration and want to get the foundations right, a conversation about scope and sequence costs nothing.

Six months later, someone needs to reproduce a result.

The code and model weights exist. But which dataset version, container image, CUDA and PyTorch versions, GPU type and distributed configuration produced the result? Was the job resumed from a checkpoint? Where are the audit records?

Reproducibility across environments means:

  • Digest-pinned container images with exact versions of CUDA, cuDNN, PyTorch and all dependencies. Not latest tags.
  • Recorded hardware configuration. A model trained on 8×H200 with InfiniBand may produce different results on 8×A100 with Ethernet, even with the same code and data.
  • Versioned data. The exact training data used, not a pointer to a mutable dataset.
  • Experiment tracking. Weights & Biases, MLflow or equivalent, configured to record hyperparameters, metrics, hardware and code version from every environment where experiments run.
  • Unified audit trails. Who launched what, when, where, with what access. The combination of provider audit logs and your own experiment tracking creates the full record.

No provider solves reproducibility for you. It is entirely customer-side and gets harder, not easier, when you add a second compute environment.

The second provider reveals what you really built.

Suppose your first integration succeeds. Six months later, another team wants different GPUs. A new provider offers better capacity, a more suitable region or a price that changes the economics.

Do you repeat the entire exercise? Another identity configuration. Another deployment process. Another monitoring system. Another cost report. Another operational burden.

Or can you extend what you've already built?

The advantage isn't access to five GPU clouds. It's being able to introduce the next one without rebuilding your engineering organisation around it.

This doesn't mean every workload should be portable. Large training jobs depend on particular hardware, interconnects, storage and data locality. Moving them indiscriminately can destroy the savings you hoped to achieve. What should be reusable is your organisation's ability to evaluate, approve, deploy, observe and operate suitable new infrastructure.

Know what to buy, what to integrate and what not to build.

The turning point is recognising that your organisation doesn't need another independent set of identities, deployment tools, dashboards and runbooks. It needs to extend capabilities it already trusts into the new environment. Your enterprise identity remains authoritative. Researchers retain familiar workflows. Finance can attribute costs. Operations has an end-to-end view. Provider-specific features remain available where they make a genuine difference.

Separate the work into three categories rather than calling entire technical areas solved or unsolved:

Adopt established capabilities. Kubernetes scheduling, SAML or OIDC federation, VPNs, experiment tracking and provider monitoring have established tools and patterns. Verify which are available and supported for the selected service.

Integrate to your enterprise. Group-to-role mapping, cross-cloud workload credentials, approved data paths, common job ownership, billing correlation, audit-log ingestion and incident escalation are specific to your architecture and policies.

Make explicit trade-offs on hard problems. Stateful workload migration, terabyte-scale checkpoints, GPU topology differences, cross-provider capacity fallback and reproducibility cannot be assumed away by a portable job specification.

Do not build a universal multi-cloud platform before you have a real placement problem. Equally, do not accept a first workload that only works because a researcher manually configured credentials and an engineer knows which undocumented steps to repeat.

Get one real workload into production. Then make the next one easier.

The opposite mistake is to recognise this challenge and design an elaborate multi-cloud platform before anyone has run a useful workload. Start with one workload and a production acceptance test that protects the original investment case.

  1. Choose the workload that justifies the move. Lower completed-job cost? Faster experiments? Available capacity? Newer hardware? An approved processing location? Establish a baseline, an owner and a measurable success criterion.
  2. Resolve what could stop it. Identity, security, networking, data and residency requirements — while they can still influence provider selection and contract terms.
  3. Connect it to your existing operating model. Reuse established developer tools, access controls, monitoring and incident response. Build the missing integration, not another organisation.
  4. Prove the result under real conditions. Run the actual workload. Measure useful throughput, total cost, time to production, reliability and the effort required to support it. Test access revocation, failure recovery and incident escalation.
  5. Keep what makes the next move easier. Turn proven identity patterns, job interfaces, cost attribution and operational practices into reusable capabilities. Add wider orchestration only when demand warrants it.

The first milestone isn't "we have a multi-cloud platform." It's "our researchers can run this workload securely, economically and reliably, and our existing team can support it."

The next infrastructure advantage is the ability to absorb change.

New GPU hardware will arrive. Capacity and pricing will move. Providers will expand their regions and enterprise services. Those developments create opportunity only for organisations that can act on them.

If every new provider requires months of engineering, your choices are constrained by your integration backlog. If you have a proven way to introduce compute, you can choose based on the workload, the economics and the business requirement. The victory is concrete: researchers have usable capacity, security has an approved architecture, finance can demonstrate the workload economics and the existing platform team can support the environment.

The GPUs are the purchase. The ability to put them to work inside your business is the asset.
